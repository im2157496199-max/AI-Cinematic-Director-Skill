# In the Blink of an Eye — Walter Murch — Focused / Deduplicated Skill Extraction

> Source: *In the Blink of an Eye, 2nd Edition: A Perspective on Film Editing* — Walter Murch.
> Role: editorial decision logic for multi-shot AI video / manga-to-video / sequence assembly.
> Project policy: keep editing judgment, not legacy film machinery or digital-system history.

## ACTIVE CORE

### E01 — Editing is selection, order, timing, and omission
Editing decides what information appears, when it appears, what is withheld, and how long each idea lives.

### E02 — Do the most with the least
Prefer the smallest number of shots/cuts/details that can create the intended response.
Do not cut merely because another angle exists. Holding a shot is a valid choice.

### E03 — Rule of Six priority
1. Emotion
2. Story
3. Rhythm
4. Eye-trace
5. Planarity / screen-plane grammar
6. Three-dimensional spatial continuity

Aim to satisfy all six. When tradeoffs are unavoidable, preserve the higher priorities first.

### E04 — Emotion outranks technical perfection when necessary
A technically perfect cut that weakens the intended feeling is worse than an emotionally/story-correct cut with a lower-order continuity imperfection.

### E05 — A cut ends one thought and begins another
Treat cuts as punctuation between ideas, realizations, emotional states, or attentional shifts.

### E06 — Every shot has multiple potential cut points
Different cut points create different psychological effects. Choose based on what the audience has understood and what they should think next.

### E07 — Dialogue cutting follows meaning, not speaker changes
Sometimes cut to the listener before the speaker finishes. Sometimes hold on the speaker after a line to reveal subtext, hesitation, deception, or consequence.

### E08 — Blink analogy is a heuristic, not literal blink detection
A cut often feels right near a mental punctuation point. Do not implement “cut every time the actor blinks.”

### E09 — Lead the audience slightly
Give the next needed piece just before the viewer consciously asks for it.
Too early causes confusion; too late causes drag.

### E10 — Eye-trace supports attentional continuity
Track the audience's likely visual focus before and after important cuts.

### E11 — Misdirection is controlled attention
Guide attention deliberately when needed for surprise, reveal, or comprehension. Do not use it to create arbitrary confusion.

### E12 — Judge only what is on screen
Ignore sunk cost. Generation time, compute cost, prompt difficulty, or attachment to an isolated frame do not justify keeping a weak shot.

### E13 — Separate creation context from audience experience
When reviewing, temporarily forget the production pain and ask what a first-time viewer actually sees, understands, and feels.

### E14 — Alternate cuts can reveal hidden intent
Use A/B sequence versions when uncertain. Compare emotional/story consequences rather than visual smoothness only.

### E15 — Reverse storyboard / contact sheet supports editing
Use one representative frame per shot to review order, repetition, progression, missing coverage, and drift at a glance.

### E16 — Audience feedback identifies pain, not always the cause
A disliked scene may be failing because of missing setup earlier. Diagnose upstream causes before deleting the complained-about moment.

### E17 — Maintain a map when options explode
Cheap digital/AI variation can create aimless thrashing. Preserve notes, shot status, goals, and version rationale.

### E18 — Editing rhythm scales with thought/emotional density
Fast conflict may support frequent cuts; quiet observation may support fewer. Do not hard-code cuts-per-minute targets.

### E19 — Cut rate is not quality
More cuts do not automatically mean more energy, quality, or professionalism.

### E20 — Preserve sequence-level emotional rhythm
The final objective is a coherent rhythm of audience feeling and thought across the whole sequence.
